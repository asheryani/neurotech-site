01

Where agents pay off in procurement

Every purchase runs through the same unstructured inputs: a request in an email, three quotes in three formats, a supplier's certificates as scans. Agents read all of it.

Procurement departments are measured on savings, compliance and supplier performance, but much of the day goes to administration: classifying incoming requests, chasing missing information, drafting requests for quotation, normalising quotes that arrive as PDFs and spreadsheets, checking supplier documents and following up on order confirmations. The strategic part of the job, negotiating and managing the supply base, gets what is left.

AI agents are unusually well suited to this function because the inputs are unstructured and the rules are clear. A request either falls under an existing framework agreement or it does not; a quote either meets the specification or deviates; a supplier's certificate is either valid or expired. An agent can determine this from documents and emails, prepare the comparison and the recommendation, and leave the award to the buyer.

Compliance requirements, from four-eyes approval thresholds to supply-chain due-diligence obligations, are not an obstacle to this. They define exactly where the human checkpoints go, and an agent that logs every step makes them easier to demonstrate, not harder.

02

Use cases

Seven workflows we see procurement teams start with, from request intake to supplier communication.

i.

Purchase-request intake and classification

Requests arrive by email, chat or form in every possible shape. The agent extracts what is needed, checks it against catalogues and framework agreements, asks the requester for missing details and creates a complete requisition in the ERP. Buyers see clean requests instead of conversations.

ii.

Supplier discovery and RFQ drafting

For requests without a contracted supplier, the agent shortlists candidates from your supplier base and approved sources, drafts the request for quotation from the specification and your templates, and sends it after the buyer's approval. Responses are tracked and reminders sent automatically.

iii.

Quote comparison and normalisation

Quotes come back as PDFs, spreadsheets and emails with different units, currencies, delivery terms and options. The agent extracts them into one comparison table, flags deviations from the specification and calculates total cost including freight and payment terms. The buyer decides the award.

iv.

Supplier onboarding and document checks

New suppliers submit registration forms, certificates, insurance and compliance declarations. The agent checks completeness and validity dates, screens against sanction and risk lists you subscribe to, and prepares the onboarding file including supply-chain due-diligence documentation. Procurement and compliance approve.

v.

Contract and price-list monitoring

The agent monitors framework agreements for expiry and volume thresholds, compares invoiced prices with contracted prices, and watches supplier price-list updates for changes that affect your parts. Buyers receive exceptions, not reports.

vi.

Spend analysis and maverick-buying detection

Across ERP, credit-card and invoice data, the agent classifies spend, identifies purchases outside contracts and channels, and prepares the quarterly spend review with savings hypotheses. Category managers validate and prioritise.

vii.

Order confirmation and delivery follow-ups

The agent reads order confirmations, compares quantities, prices and dates with the purchase order, updates the ERP where everything matches and flags deviations. Late or partial deliveries trigger a drafted follow-up to the supplier that the buyer releases.

A worked example

From requisition to award: the RFQ workflow

This is the workflow most procurement teams choose first, because it combines high volume with a clear approval structure. It covers indirect and direct purchases below the threshold for a formal tender.

  1. A requester describes what they need in the request form or by email. The agent structures the request, checks whether a framework agreement or catalogue item applies, and, if not, confirms the specification and budget holder with the requester.
  2. The agent proposes three to five suppliers from the approved supplier base, with their performance history and current documents. The buyer confirms or adjusts the shortlist.
  3. The agent drafts the RFQ from the specification, the standard terms and the deadline, and sends it after the buyer's release. Replies, questions and clarifications are tracked in one thread per supplier.
  4. As quotes arrive in different formats, the agent extracts prices, quantities, lead times, payment and delivery terms and options into a single comparison, normalised to total landed cost. Deviations from the specification are highlighted with the supplier's wording.
  5. The agent prepares an award recommendation with the reasoning: price, lead time, supplier performance, risk. The buyer reviews and decides. Above the four-eyes threshold, a second approver signs off in the e-procurement or ERP workflow.
  6. After the award, the agent creates the purchase order in the ERP, informs unsuccessful suppliers with a drafted message the buyer releases, and files the complete documentation, so any later audit can see who decided what and why.

The buyer's involvement shrinks to four decisions: shortlist, release RFQ, award, release messages. Everything between those decisions is prepared, documented and logged by the agent.

04

Guardrails for procurement

  • No autonomous ordering above thresholds

    The agent creates requisitions, drafts orders and prepares awards. Commitments above your defined thresholds require buyer approval and, where your policy says so, a second approver. Thresholds are configured per category and enforced in the workflow, not in a prompt.

  • Four-eyes principle preserved

    Existing segregation of duties between requesting, approving and ordering stays intact. The agent never holds the rights to do all three, and its actions are attributed to it in the audit log, not to a human user.

  • Supplier data protection

    Supplier contacts and documents are personal and confidential data. Retention, access and hosting follow your data-protection rules, and the agent only stores what the process needs.

  • Sanction and compliance screening stays a human decision

    The agent screens and flags; a compliance officer decides on matches. Supply-chain due-diligence obligations are documented in the onboarding file for review, not concluded by the agent.

  • Full audit trail

    Every extraction, comparison and recommendation is logged with source documents and model version. Internal audit and external auditors can trace an award end to end.

Frequently asked questions

Does the agent work with our ERP and e-procurement system?

Most likely. SAP, Microsoft Dynamics, Oracle and the common e-procurement platforms expose APIs for requisitions, purchase orders and supplier master data, and supplier portals can usually be integrated by email or file exchange where no API exists. We verify the concrete systems during the assessment and tell you where integration is simple and where it needs work.

How does the agent handle quotes in different formats?

It reads PDFs, spreadsheets, emails and scanned documents, extracts the line items and terms, and normalises units, currencies and delivery conditions into your comparison template. Where a quote is ambiguous, it asks the supplier or flags the item for the buyer rather than guessing. Extraction quality is measured on your own past quotes during the pilot.

Can the agent negotiate with suppliers?

We do not recommend it in the first phases. The agent prepares negotiation briefs with price history, alternatives and target prices; the buyer negotiates. Automated messages to suppliers, such as reminders and confirmations, are drafted by the agent and released by the buyer until the team decides otherwise.

What about supply-chain due-diligence and other compliance duties?

The agent helps collect, check and document supplier information, including the declarations and certificates your compliance programme requires, and flags gaps and expiries. The compliance assessment itself remains a human decision. This is process support, not legal advice.

How is supplier data protected?

Supplier contacts, quotes and contracts are handled under your data-protection and confidentiality rules: scoped access, EU hosting where required, defined retention and a full log of what the agent accessed. Our governance service documents this for your DPO.

What happens to the buyers' jobs?

The administrative share of the job shrinks and the strategic share grows: more categories actively managed, more negotiations prepared properly, more supplier development. Where the change is significant, we plan it with the team and HR rather than around them.
